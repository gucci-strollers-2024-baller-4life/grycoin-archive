# ¶238 names one paragraph, and ¶241 changes what the initials are

**Date:** 2026-10-31
**Base:** `wp_paras_br.json`, mixed, md5 `bd07e6ee…`, self-check clean.
**Script:** `sweeps/i_anchor_audit.py`, results in `sweeps/i_anchor_audit_results.json`.
**Derivations run:** none. `dd0740d` already derived the four anchors, the pentads and the unions;
nothing here repeats that. This entry covers three things that pass does not: which paragraph ¶238
actually names, ¶241, and two textual edits inside the instruction block.

## 1. ¶238 names its paragraph by content, and there is only one

¶238:

> *"Combined with the I as the first letter in the paragraphs close to that. The paragraph containing
> the words 'I made an attempt to make my own proof-of-work based currency, called Bitcoin.'"*

That sentence is ¶234 (`hal_original.txt` ¶5, *"I was more positive…"*), and the chapter says out loud
that it has been rewritten — ¶219: *"And that is actually very close to outing himself. Just remove a
couple of words and you get 'I made an attempt to make my own proof-of-work based currency, called
Bitcoin.'"* (Hal wrote `RPOW`; the chapter's version says `Bitcoin`.)

So the operative `I` is the interleaved paragraph's initial, pinned by a quoted sentence, not by
position. The three lead-ins also start with `I`, but that is a 47/273 base rate, and ¶238 does not
point at them. Since the pentad reading is now derived negative, this changes no result. It does mean
the sentence "the I as the first letter in the paragraphs close to that" is doing disambiguation work
in the text: there are two `I` paragraphs near the Hal block (hal ¶1 before it, hal ¶5 inside it), and
¶238 names the second one.

## 2. ¶241, which no pass has used

> *"And by the way, the FF and WW has meaning as well, as a dedication message. WW obviously is UUUU
> or 'four U, for you'. I leave it as an exercise for the reader to figure out who FF is."*

This paragraph says the initials are **content**, not geometry:

- `W` is "double-U", so `WW` is four `U`s, which is "for you": a dedication.
- `FF` is explicitly left open, by the author, in the text.

Two consequences worth taking seriously.

First, `FFWW` as a *search key* was the wrong object from the start. Every sweep so far has treated the
initials as a pattern to be matched somewhere else in the chapter. If the author is telling the truth
here, they are a dedication and a termination rule, and the chapter-wide census finding no fifth
`FFWW`/`stnm` site is not a failed search, it is the expected outcome. That is a reason the geometry
programme kept returning zero, and it is textual, not statistical.

Second, "who FF is" is a live question with the author's own framing attached, and it is cheap to
answer because a two-`F` name is a small search over the material already in the repo. The obvious
candidate in Hal Finney's surroundings is his wife Fran Finney, but I am flagging that as a hypothesis
to verify from a source, not as a finding. If `FF` resolves to a person, `WW` = "for you" makes the
pair a dedication addressed by one party to another, and that names which party engineered the four
paragraphs — Hal, or the author of the chapter.

## 3. Two edits inside the instruction block

**(a) `true` → `real`.** The chapter quotes hal ¶7 verbatim at ¶222: *"Today, Satoshi's **true**
identity has become a mystery…"*. The fragment at ¶243 reads *"Today, Satoshi's **real** identity"*,
and ¶246 states the extracted message as *"Satoshi's **real** identity, recognize the signs"*.

Chapter-wide: `true identity` once (¶222, the verbatim quote), `real identity` twice (¶243 and ¶246,
both in the instruction block). So ¶243 is not a quotation, it is an edit, and the edit is
load-bearing: the acrostic only reads as a sentence if the word is `real`.

And `Satoshi's Real Identity` is the title of a post in this corpus (`wparts.json`, id 724030807). The
extraction the author demonstrates at ¶242–¶246 therefore terminates in **a post title**, not in a key:

```
¶242  The first four and last four words of the paragraph hinting at the solution.
¶243  "Today, Satoshi's real identity"      <- 4 words
¶244  "I recognize the signs."              <- 4 words
¶245  -> first words: "Today, I"
¶246  -> next three words: "Satoshi's real identity, recognize the signs"
```

Column-wise over two four-word halves: word 1 of each, then words 2–4 of each. She edited a word to
make that read, and what it reads is the name of another document.

**(b) `facinating` → `fascinating`.** `hal_original.txt` ¶5 has *"So I found Bitcoin **facinating**."*
The chapter's reprint ¶234 has *"**fascinating**"*. The reprint is a re-typeset copy, not a byte copy,
which is a qualification to calling W4 a 0.99 quotation of the source file.

It does **not** touch the sweeps: `sweeps/sixteen_matrix.py` and `sweeps/ft_closure.py` both read
`wp_paras_br.json` and mark the chapter's own paragraphs, so W4 was always marked from ¶231–¶235, never
from `hal_original.txt`. The divergence only matters to a script that substitutes the source file for
the reprint. Both spellings end in `g`, so no letter-level result moves.

## 4. Two corrections to the census, both conceded

**(a) The `F…t` census says containment, not uniqueness.** `SELECTOR_CENSUS_2026-10-31.md` wrote that
no fifth site exists "contiguous or scattered". The contiguous half is right; the scattered half is
wrong, and `dd0740d` shows why: the `s`/`n`/`m` members can be drawn from anywhere, so 61 scattered
sites exist, each passing through one of the four `F…t` paragraphs. The correct statement is
**containment**: every site of the signature must include ¶6, ¶94, ¶169 or ¶232, and all 61 have now
been derived negative. The census doc is corrected inline.

**(b) The out-of-class count is 118, not 121, and my 152 was measuring a different convention.**
`dd0740d` §4 has the right inventory: `ITASM` covers 154 case-folded paragraphs, leaving 118 with
letters, or 119 counting the one paragraph with no letters at all. My census's 152 came from *not*
folding the ten lettered subheadings (`a)` … `f)`, at ¶176, ¶182, ¶209, ¶215, ¶250, ¶252, ¶254, ¶256,
¶258, ¶260), which pushed six letters into a lowercase bucket of their own. Adopting 118/119.

## Verdict

Nothing here reopens the derivation space, and nothing here should be read as a new selector. What it
does is retire the premise: the author states in ¶241 that her initials are a dedication with a
recipient, and she demonstrates at ¶242–¶246 that a demonstrated extraction in this chapter ends in
another document's title. Both point at the letters being read rather than matched, which is the one
reading the sweeps have never been able to test.
